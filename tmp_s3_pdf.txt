A Lightweight Framework for Fast Trajectory Simpliﬁcation
Ziquan Fang†, Changhao He †, Lu Chen †, Danlei Hu †, Qichen Sun †, Linsen Li ‡, Y unjun Gao†
†Zhejiang University, Zhejiang Province, China
‡Hikvision Research Institute, Zhejiang Province, China

Abstract—The ubiquitous GPS sensors collect massive tra-
jectory data from moving objects, which is useful in data
mining applications. However, trajectory data is enormous in
volume, and thus, directly storing and processing the raw data
is expensive. Using trajectory simpliﬁcation , a trajectory can be
reduced to a set of continuous line segments with acceptable data
loss, which is an efﬁcient method. Although many algorithms are
proposed, they still suffer from the following issues including (i)
non-data driven capability as most studies rely on human-crafted
rules or pre-deﬁned parameters, (ii) bound with error measures
that yield high computational cost, and (iii) focusing only on
the local information preservation in trajectories, but failing in
capturing the global mobility patterns for trajectory compression.
To address the above issues, we propose a Seq2Seq2Seq
framework, abbreviated S3, which consists of two chained
Seq2Seq. With differentiable reconstruction learning, S3 enables
self-supervised trajectory simpliﬁcation in a lightweight manner .
Besides, we deploy S3 over the graph neural architecture to
capture the context-aware mobility patterns and enhance the
representation paradigm of trajectories with geographical se-
mantics, where a context-aware distance measure is designed for
quality evaluation. An online extension of S3 is also developed
to enable streaming trajectory simpliﬁcations. Finally, extensive
experiments using two real-world datasets in both ofﬂine and
online scenarios show that S3 achieves much higher efﬁciency
(e.g., it achieves up to one order of magnitude speed-up gains)
and comparable compression quality, compared with both non-
learning and state-of-the-art learning-based methods.
Index Terms —Trajectory simpliﬁcation, deep representation
learning, sequence-to-sequence
I. I NTRODUCTION
With the development of techniques for positioning, GPS
sensors with high sampling rates and low prices are widely
used in smartphones, vehicles, and wearable devices. These
built-in GPS sensors produce massive trajectory data that
record the traces of moving objects (e.g., pedestrians and ve-
hicles). For example, the world’s largest ride-sharing company
Uber hits 10 billion rides in July, 2018 [1]. Based on the col-
lected trajectory data, many trajectory data mining tasks [41],
[64] such as similarity queries, clustering analytics, and trafﬁc
prediction are studied, beneﬁting a broad range of applications
in trafﬁc management [20], urban computing [65], and behav-
ior study [7]. Since trajectories capture the hidden mobility
patterns and complex interactions with the located context of
moving objects, driven by the success of machine learning,
increasing AI-based trajectory data analytics algorithms [53]
are proposed recently, yielding similarity learning [28], deep
clustering [11], and deep prediction [13]. The community has
reached a point where more and more researchers embrace AI
techniques as an integral part of trajectory analytics.
The volume of trajectory data is continuously growing in
terms of two aspects: i) higher sampling rates of sensors enable
acquiring more ﬁne-grained locations of each moving object,
which leads to longer trajectories, e.g., a single trajectory may
contain hundreds to thousands of time-stamped GPS points;
and ii) more monitored moving objects (e.g., city-wide taxis)
generate trajectory data simultaneously. Directly transmitting
and storing such massive data brings pressure on network
bandwidth and storage capacity, which also makes trajectory
mining processing expensive. Recall that redundancy com-
monly exists in trajectories. For instance, all sampling points
but the ﬁrst and last could be dropped when a vehicle travels
in a straight road segment at the same speed. Hence, the
community calls for trajectory compression [4], [9], [19], [36],
[37], [42], [54], [55] to reduce data volumes, which serves as
the cornerstone of downstream trajectory analyses.
Lossless compression is widely studied for text and images,
but it has been proved to be ineffective (low compression ra-
tios) for trajectory data, according to the experiments of previ-
ous work [8], [18], [45]. Basically, a reasonable approximation
of query results is acceptable in most real-life applications
such as hot area detection and similarity queries [14], [39].
Correspondingly, a tolerable information loss of the original
trajectory is acceptable, as long as the query results based on
that are not deviated too far. Thus, many research efforts have
been devoted to lossy compression. Trajectory simpliﬁcation,
which preserves the remaining points of a simpliﬁed trajectory
while dropping some points from a raw trajectory, is now the
most common lossy compression method, offering a much
higher compression ratio with acceptable data loss. Existing
trajectory simpliﬁcation algorithms fall into two categories: (i)
min-#: it generates a simpliﬁed trajectory with errors bounded
in ϵand as few as trajectory points based on a raw trajectory
with n points and (ii) min- ϵ: given a trajectory with n points
and a positive integer m, it ﬁnds a subsequence consisting
of m points to approximate this trajectory and attempts to
minimize the compression error. Speciﬁcally, we study min- ϵ
oriented trajectory simpliﬁcation as it is convenient to control
the compression ratio (i.e., m
n ) and especially effective for
resource-constrained sensors [31], [62].
Quite a few algorithms exist for min- ϵtrajectory simpliﬁ-
cation. In the early stage, optimal (or called exact) algorithms
such as Bellman [4] and Error-Search [37] are proposed. The
exact algorithms typically have a high complexity, making
them impractical for large-scale trajectory analytics. As a
result, approximate algorithms are more preferred, which can

of low efﬁciency. In terms of non-learning-based methods,
they are closely coupled with distance-based error metrics,

of learning-based methods, although RLTS achieves better

thousands of seconds. Considering massive trajectories in a
dataset required to be compressed, the processing time will
increase linearly. Moreover, according to our experimental
study, RLTS needs a lot of time for model training before
performing one simpliﬁcation request. Such expensive time
costs restrain existing methods from time-aware applications

Another limitation of existing methods is, they typically
focus on local characteristic preservation while ignoring the
context-aware global mobility. The local mobility dependence
considers the features of intra-trajectory such as position
information [44] and direction information [36]. However, the
local dependence cannot effectively capture the interaction
between trajectories and its located environment, i.e., the
global mobility dependence. As shown in Fig. 1 (left), there
are some semantic-aware POIs in a long trajectory that should
be extracted to decide its context-aware moving trend. The
non-learning DP algorithm guided by existing error metrics
generates different results, i.e., TP and TS, as each metric
considers a different perspective ofT. More importantly, those
error metrics focus on local spatial information preservation,
resulting in coarse resolution with global mobility trend loss.
In contrast, S3 can identify the POI points during trajectory
simpliﬁcation process. Notice that, there is another bunch of
studies called semantic trajectory compression, which lever-
ages external activities or hand-extracted features to facilitate
trajectory compression by activity/map matching [14], [15]
or clustering [35]. It is not the scope of this paper, as the
activity/road features are not always available.
In contrast to previous studies that rely on manual rules or
heavyweight reinforcement learning, in this paper, we utilize
the lightweight sequence representation learning for trajectory
simpliﬁcation, which means that it chooses which point to drop
based on its learned weight during representation learning.
This is the ﬁrst proposal that studies trajectory simpliﬁcation
that trains lightweight neural networks and performs end-
to-end trajectory simpliﬁcation, to the best of our knowl-
edge. However, incorporating representation learning into the
trajectory simpliﬁcation task is not an easy task. Existing
deep learning models rely on sufﬁcient labels in a supervised
manner. In trajectory simpliﬁcation, practical scenarios may
involve a very limited amount of simpliﬁed trajectories due
to expensive human efforts. Besides, different error metrics
also result in different simpliﬁed results (see Fig. 1), i.e., it is
impossible to collect ground truth for trajectory simpliﬁcation.
We propose a self-supervised trajectory simpliﬁcation
framework, abbreviated S3, to address the limitations of ex-
isting methods and the aforementioned difﬁculties. The input

context-aware graph neural network that is integrated into S3

the efﬁciency of compressed trajectories. Besides, we present
a new error distance for quality evaluation. Last but not the
least, owning to the lightweight architecture of S3, we extend
S3 to enable online trajectory simpliﬁcation processing.
In conclusion, the following contributions are made.
• We present the ﬁrst deep learning based framework for
fast trajectory simpliﬁcation. The proposed S3 i) is data-
driven, ii) runs in a lightweight way without the supervi-
sion of labeled data or error computation, and iii) achieves
much better efﬁciency and competitive effectiveness.
• We design a Sequence-to-Sequence-to-Sequence architec-
ture that consists of a Compressor and a Constructor,
enabling fast trajectory simpliﬁcation in a self-supervised
manner. A context-aware GNN is further build to preserve
both local and global mobility dependencies.
• We extend S3 for streaming settings, so that online
trajectory simpliﬁcation tasks can be also supported.
• We compare S3 with both non-learning and learning
based baselines in ofﬂine and online modes, and the
results show that S3 is much more efﬁcient (e.g., it
achieves up to one order of magnitude speed up gains)
and also effective (e.g., it yields competitive quality).

A. Ofﬂine and Online Trajectory Simpliﬁcation
Trajectory simpliﬁcation is inspired by graphic geome-
try [56], which roughly fall into two categories: ofﬂine and
online approaches to trajectory simpliﬁcation.
In ofﬂine settings, the collected trajectories are static and
completely available. We only review algorithms about min-
ϵ, while the min- # studies [25], [30], [32]–[34], [36], [44]
are omitted since they are not the scope of this paper. The
Bellman [4] approximation is regarded as the ﬁrst algorithm
for simplifying a trajectory. It aims to generate a subsequence,
which has m points with the smallest error, using dynamic
programming. The exact algorithm has a high complexity
O(n3). Then, Meratnia et al. [9] introduce the classic Douglas-
Peucker (DP) algorithm in a Top-Down manner [19].O(n2)
is the worst Top-Down time complexity. To improve the efﬁ-
ciency, another implementation of DP called Bottom-Up [36]
is proposed. Only the positional information of trajectories
is saved by aforementioned methods. To preserve direction
information of trajectories, Long et al. [37] develop an exact
algorithm (called Error-Search) and an approximate algo-
rithm (called Span-Search), both of which use their speciﬁed
DAD (direction-aware error metric). When deciding which
point to drop, all of these algorithms rely on human-made
rules. As AI techniques are propagated in trajectory analyses,
Wang et al. [54] use reinforcement learning to develop the
ﬁrst learning-based trajectory simpliﬁcation. Despite their ef-
fectiveness, reinforcement learning brings expensive training
costs, highly constraining their deployments.In contrast, S3 is
data-driven but is more lightweight that utilizes self-supervised
learning requiring no rules or massive computations, enabling
fast trajectory simpliﬁcation.
Remote sensors continuously collect and store streaming
trajectory data in a buffer (also known as a mini-batch) in
online environments. Whenever the buffer becomes full, online
trajectory simpliﬁcation decides which points to drop. Based
on that, a large number of methods are proposed in the
literature [22], [26], [30], [32], [33], [42]–[44], [47], [52].
On the trajectory data streams, these two methods are local
optimization techniques. Their main advantage is their high
efﬁciency, but the quality of their simpliﬁcations may decrease
signiﬁcantly [62]. Again, the majority of these strategies are
based on rules made by humans. Wang et al. [55] utilize
reinforcement learning in an online setting. In this paper , we
are motivated to solve the ofﬂine and online min-ϵtrajectory
simpliﬁcation tasks without hurting simpliﬁcation quality.

B. Other Types of Trajectory Compression
The vehicle’s trajectory is restricted by a road network, the
road network space is utilized rather than free space. Thus,
[5], [21], [23], [27], [51] use road network’s structural char-
acteristics to simplify the trajectory. Since any map-matching
algorithm can map a local sequence of successive points to the
same road segment, road network-based trajectory simpliﬁca-
tion reduces spatial redundancy. Frequent travel path mining of
people and vehicles can be utilized to reduce the data volumes.
However, the above methods are low efﬁciency due to the
map-matching algorithm. There is also another line of studies
called semantic trajectory simpliﬁcation, which replaces orig-
inal GPS trajectories with semantic-based trajectories, where
the semantic means activities [48], stop points [35] or other
events [58]. As summarized in [62], in that case, It deletes
all points in the moving state and only keeps the location
points that represent semantic meanings. As a result, accuracy
in simpliﬁcation cannot be guaranteed. For the two reasons
listed below, the road network or semantic-based trajectory
compression algorithms are outside the scope. First, additional
map-matching or semantic knowledge is required for these
methods, which is not always available. [54], [62]. Second,
rather than using the original trajectory, these techniques are
applied on top of transformed trajectories like road segments
or sequential context.In this paper , we focus on free space, we
concentrate on simplifying trajectory simpliﬁcation for people,
vehicles, animals, etc., which has been the subject of a lot of
research in the literature.

IV . BASIC S3 APPROACH
The rationale behind min-ϵis that important points in this
trajectory typically contribute more to both the representation
and reconstruction process for neural network training.
As depicted in Fig. 4, S3 consists of four modules, i.e.,
Embedding layer, Compressor, Constructor, and Differential
layer, highlighted by different colors. The Embedding layer
transforms raw data (i.e., positions) into discrete tokens (i.e.,
vectors) for neural networks, i.e.,pi →gi, as neural networks
only adapt vectors as inputs. The Compressor takes a trajectory
T as an input and produces a simpliﬁed trajectoryT′, while the
Constructor tries to recover the input asˆT from the simpliﬁed

E. Complexity Analyses
The complexity of basic S3 approach is O(n). Here, n
denotes the length of a target trajectory. Speciﬁcally, the
time is dominated by two components, i.e., Compressor and
Constructor. As both components own the same Seq2Seq,
we only need to analyze one of them, whose time relies on
the Seq2Seq and Attention. We analyze them as follows. In
Seq2Seq, the time cost for one original trajectory consists of (i)
that of obtaining embeddings of points, which isO(1), and (ii)
that of obtaining the deep representation via a bi-RNN, which
is O(1). In Attention layer, the time cost for position weight
computation is O(n). Therefore, the time complexity of S3
is O(1) × O(n) = O(n). Note that the O(n) time complexity
maintains the current best time complexity as those of existing
algorithms for the trajectory simpliﬁcation task.

A. Graph Construction
To capture the above global mobility dependence, a typical
way of previous studies [12], [17] is to use prior road network
or POI knowledge. However, such external information is not
always available in real-life applications. In contrast, we utilize
raw trajectories to construct the spatial graph to capture the
global dependence through capturing the correlations among
spatial locations in all trajectories. Speciﬁcally, we construct
a context-aware spatial graph G =( V,E), where each node
in G denotes a grid cell, and edges indicate the connectivity
between grid cells. As depicted in Fig. 6, an edge is created
between grid cell gi and grid cell gj if a trajectory contains
a consecutive snippet from gi\gj to gj\gi. In high-sampling
trajectories, some consecutive spatio-temporal points may be
mapped to the same grid cell. In this case, we remove the self-
loop edges in G. The weight on edge ei,j ∈E is deﬁned as
the number of trajectories that contain the consecutive snippet
(gi\gj ↔gj\gi). Furthermore, the semantic context of nodes
can be expressed by a feature matrix. Note that, in this paper,
we adopt a one-hot vector (the length equals to the number
of grid cells) for each node gi to express the uniqueness of
its location in the feature matrix. We use A andX to denote
adjacency matrix and feature matrix.

VI. O NLINE EXTENSION OF S3
In online settings, trajectory points that have been dropped
will no longer be accessible, where the trajectory that needs
to be simpliﬁed is arriving point by point. Applications like
remote sensing, where the sensors must work within a limited
storage budget, network bandwidth, and energy, frequently
employ this mode. However, it is non-trivial to directly deploy
S3 into online settings as the biggest difference between
ofﬂine and online settings is the high-efﬁciency requirements
of the latter case. According to the complexity analyses of
ofﬂine S3 approach, it is inefﬁcient to directly deploy S3 in
online settings. To this end, we propose an online extension
of S3, which features two modiﬁcations. First, when each
point passes the Compressor, we cache its embedding into
a hash table, which can be directly connected with next point

A. Experimental Settings
Datasets. We use two kinds of real datasets to determine
whether an algorithm is adaptable, considering the character-

mode of transportation of moving objects.

of more than three years, including multiple kinds of

• T-drive2 contains the trajectories of 10,357 taxis in

AI techniques (i.e., reinforcement learning) to achieve state-
of-the-art data-driven trajectory simpliﬁcation for ofﬂine and
online modes, which is also an approximate algorithm. Note
that, we use PED, SED, DAD, and CED for algorithm quality
evaluation, and use running time for algorithm efﬁciency eval-
uation. S3 can process trajectories in groups like Trajstore [8]
due to its batch processing.

of the fact that after repeating each comparison method twenty
times, we present the average performance. The units of SED,
PED, and DAD are 10m, 10m, and 1 radian, respectively. For
different compression ratios under the same dataset, there is no
need to train S3 for each compression ratio. In S3, the length
of the encoder of Compressor and the length of the decoder of
Constructor refer to the length of the input trajectory; while the
length of the decoder of the Compressor and the length of the
encoder of the Constructor refer to the length of the simpliﬁed
trajectory. Note that, these length parameters can be ﬂexibly
set to any value. We train S3 under the default ratio (i.e., 0.3).
Once S3 is well-trained for model inference, we adjust the
length of the decoder of the Compressor, and the length of the
encoder of the Constructor, for different ratios. Such a process
is the same as general Seq2Seq training [24]. For different
datasets, we train S3, respectively, as different datasets may
exhibit different mobility features that are required to be
learned to guide the model training. Python and PyTorch were
used in the framework’s implementation. Last but not least, all
of the experiments were carried out on a server equipped with
a GeForce GTX-2080 Ti 11G GPU, an Intel Silver 4210R
processor running at 2.40 GHz, and 16 GB of RAM.

lightweight processing, S3 has the most stable performance.
The second observation is that S3 performs better than all
learning-based and non-learning methods, particularly when
trajectories are longer, indicating high efﬁciency. For example,
for trajectories with about 35 (x102) points under DAD error

and 4 times faster than RLTS (about 6s). Such high efﬁciency
enables S3 to support efﬁcient downstream trajectory analysis,
as trajectory compression is a pre-processing step in the
trajectory data management pipeline. Other observations are
summarized: (i) Error-Search requiresO(n2logn) complexity
and runs timeout (10,000+ seconds). (ii) The learning-based
RLTS cannot run faster than classic algorithms, as reinforce-
ment learning relies on decision interactions to make many
decisions, introducing additional computational costs.

D. Case Study
To demonstrate that the effectiveness gap between S3 and
existing methods has no impact, we conduct case studies.
Speciﬁcally, we evaluate the quality of the trajectory dataset
after simpliﬁcation processing. That is, we evaluate the effect
of trajectory simpliﬁcation on downstream trajectory analysis
tasks, conﬁrming the resulted errors (i.e., data loss) of both S3
and baselines are acceptable. Following previous studies [54],
[62], we select the top- k trajectory similarity query as the
evaluation task, since similarity evaluation is the most essen-
tial task for trajectory analytics, which is most suitable to
reﬂect the impact of raw data changes on analysis results.
Speciﬁcally, we utilize top-k similarity search for validation,
where we use two widely used trajectory similarity metrics,
the point-based DTW [61] and the shape-based Frechet [40].
Due to space limitation, we use DTW to verify error effect of
algorithms using PED and SED, while use Frechet to evaluate
error effect of algorithms using DAD and CED.
We adopt HR@50 as a performance metric. HR@50 denotes
the top-50 hitting ratio that captures the degree of overlap
between top-50 search results returned from a raw trajectory
dataset and that returned from a simpliﬁed trajectory dataset.
The HR@50 approaches to 1, the less the error effect on
downstream tasks. Fig. 11 plots the results. Here, we use T-
drive dataset due to similar observations on GeoLife dataset.
As observed, under the same ratios, S3 yields nearly the same
or better HR@50 (i.e., 0.80–0.96) to the baselines that show
0.42–0.98 HR@50. This demonstrates that, although S3 cannot
defeat all baselines on effectiveness, the quality gap is totally
acceptable, as S3 and baselines show the equivalent effect on
the downstream trajectory analysis tasks.
E. Model Analysis
We ﬁrst evaluate the training cost of S3, which is important
for deployment. We compare S3 with RLTS, as RLTS is the
one and the only that utilizes AI techniques. The training
times and CED errors of RLTS and S3 on T-drive are shown
in Fig. 12. Due to the large-scale computation space of
reinforcement learning, we ﬁnd that RLTS takes several hours
to train a model. In contrast, S3 only takes several minutes for
model training, which signiﬁcantly improves the usability in
real-life applications. Besides, S3 achieves comparable quality
with RLTS, due to its data-driven ability.
We then evaluate the memory usage of S3. Here, we use
the CED metric as an illustration due to similar observations
on other error distances. Fig. 13 shows the running memory
usage of S3 and RTLS by varying the compression ratio
on GeoLife and T-drive datasets. As observed, S3 occupies
nearly half fewer memory usage than RLTS, as S3 is a very
light representation learning based unsupervised framework.
In contrast, RLTS relies on reinforcement learning and has to
make massive decision computations.

VIII. C ONCLUSIONS
Existing trajectory simpliﬁcation approaches mostly rely on
human rules or pre-deﬁned parameters, and they are closely
coupled with error metrics, resulting in low versatility, inten-
sive computation, and inefﬁciency challenges. Motivated by
these, we propose a lightweight representation leaning based
framework, abbreviated S3, to perform self-supervised trajec-
tory simpliﬁcation in an end-to-end manner. Compared with
existing algorithms, S3 is data-driven, and more importantly,
it achieves very high efﬁciency in terms of both ofﬂine and
online scenarios, while exhibiting comparable quality. Besides,
both model training and model inference are lightweight. A
semantic-based deep trajectory simpliﬁcation, like the road
network and POIs, is appealing in the future.
